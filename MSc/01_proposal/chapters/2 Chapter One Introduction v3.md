# CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background

Agriculture remains central to Kenya's economy, food security and rural livelihoods. According to the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), agriculture, forestry and fishing accounted for a provisional 23.2% of gross domestic product in 2025, and the sector grew by 3.1% that year (KNBS, 2026, pp. 26, 29). Within agriculture, dairy is an important source of employment and household income. The Kenya Dairy Board (KDB) estimates that the dairy industry contributes about 12% of agricultural gross domestic product and supports the livelihoods of an estimated 1.8 million smallholder households (KDB, 2023). These figures show why improving the productivity and profitability of smallholder dairy enterprises matters.

Despite this importance, dairy productivity remains low. KDB (2023) reports average productivity of 7 to 9 litres per cow per day and identifies poor genetics, poor feeding practices, ageing farm infrastructure and inadequate investment among the challenges facing producers. Raising milk production therefore requires more than access to improved animals: farmers also need the knowledge, resources and services to manage those animals well.

The delivery of agricultural advice faces institutional and resource constraints. The Agricultural Sector Transformation and Growth Strategy (ASTGS) estimated an average of about one extension officer to 1,000 farmers and planned to reduce this to 1:600 (Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock, Fisheries and Irrigation [MoALFI], 2019, p. 140). The later Kenya Agricultural Sector Extension Policy (KASEP) notes that the ratio of extension staff to farmers has not improved, and retains a target of one extension officer to 600 farmers by 2029 (Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock Development [MoALD], 2023). The policy also identifies inadequate extension capacity, weak research, extension and clientele linkages, and coordination challenges as barriers to effective service delivery. These are sector-wide concerns rather than a measured staffing ratio for livestock extension specifically.

Digital communication offers an additional route for delivering agricultural information and supporting farmer decisions. Mobile-based advisory services can send information, reminders and answers to farmers' questions without an extension officer visiting every farm. Access to information, however, does not necessarily lead to its use. In their review of digital agricultural advisory interventions, Fabregas et al. (2019) estimated average yield gains of about 4% and a 22% increase in the odds of adopting recommended inputs. Their findings suggest that digital advice can improve agricultural outcomes, but that its benefits depend on the relevance, delivery and use of the information provided.

DigiCow is a Kenyan digital livestock service that combines information provision with farm record-keeping and links to livestock service providers. According to its product documentation, farmers can record milk production, milk sales, feeding, breeding and animal health information, access training content and obtain reports to support farm decisions. Its related Ndume application allows veterinary service providers to digitise diagnosis, treatment, vaccination, deworming and artificial insemination records, and the system generates reminders for key reproductive and management events (DigiCow Africa Ltd., n.d.). DigiCow should therefore be understood as a service that offers several information and support channels, rather than only as a smartphone application.

The proposed relationship between digital advisory and dairy outcomes involves several connected steps. Farmers must first access and understand information, and then apply relevant recommendations. Changes in feeding, preventive care, health monitoring and reproductive management may then contribute to production and income outcomes. Other possible channels include better record-keeping, better planning and reduced waste. In this study, these relationships form a proposed analytical pathway, not an assumption that the service directly improves cattle health or guarantees higher income.

Kenya's policy framework supports the study of such services. KASEP recognises the growing importance of information and digital technologies and the need for coordinated public and private extension provision, with emphasis on knowledge management, partnerships and approaches that respond to farmers' needs (MoALD, 2023). More recently, the draft Kenya Agricultural Data, Information and Digital Policy of August 2026 signals further policy attention to agricultural data and digitally enabled services, although it remains a draft (MoALD, 2026). Evaluating digital livestock advisory is therefore relevant to decisions on how digital services can complement existing extension systems.

The Mercy Corps AgriFin Digital Farmer II (ADF2) programme supported bundles of digitally enabled agricultural and financial services intended to improve smallholder productivity, income and resilience (Mercy Corps AgriFin, n.d.-b). The data-sharing package available for this study covers 11 service partners in Kenya, Nigeria and Ethiopia, including farmer-level descriptive data and impact evaluation tables for five partners, one of which is DigiCow (Mercy Corps AgriFin, n.d.-a). This evaluation sample should be distinguished from the wider ADF2 programme.

The DigiCow component was evaluated by IDinsight for Mercy Corps AgriFin (IDinsight, 2026). The evaluation compared 502 DigiCow farmers with 775 comparison farmers drawn from the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) livestock registry in the same counties, using telephone interviews conducted from 7 to 27 August 2025. It provides a foundation for examining dairy outcomes, cattle health management practices and farmers' use of advisory information. The present study proposes a secondary analysis of these data, subject to obtaining the necessary microdata, documentation and permissions.

## 1.2 Statement of the problem

Digital agricultural advisory services are intended to improve farm outcomes by helping farmers make better decisions. However, an average difference in outcomes does not by itself explain which information farmers used, which practices they changed, or how those changes relate to productivity and income. The modest average yield gains reported by Fabregas et al. (2019) show the potential of digital advice, but they do not establish that every advisory service produces similar benefits or works through the same pathway.

For DigiCow, the existing evaluation already provides evidence on selected practices and economic outcomes. It reports that DigiCow farmers scored 0.38 points (8.9%) higher than comparison farmers on a six-practice cattle health index, with health monitoring and heat detection driving the difference, and it reports milk production and net income from cattle (IDinsight, 2026). DigiCow farmers produced on average 49 more litres of milk per cow per year (6.7%) and earned about KES 1,641 more from cattle per year than comparison farmers, but neither difference was statistically significant (IDinsight, 2026). Whether DigiCow improves productivity and income, and through which practices, therefore remains an open question. A higher management practice index, however, is not the same as a demonstrated reduction in disease or a clinically measured improvement in animal health. The relationship between advisory participation, management behaviour and economic outcomes therefore needs careful examination.

A further issue is the distinction between access and meaningful use. Among sampled DigiCow farmers, about 74% could access livestock advice, 33% actively used it and 17% reported changing a farming practice because of it (IDinsight, 2026). Registration or access alone therefore does not capture how far farmers engage with information or act on it. Examining information type, frequency of use and farmer characteristics may help explain differences in reported practice adoption among users.

The data package includes an attenuation analysis linking the cattle health index to income. This offers a starting point for examining an explanatory pathway, but a smaller participation coefficient after adding a possible mediator does not, on its own, establish causal mediation. Such an interpretation requires further assumptions about confounding, measurement and the time order of participation, management practices and income (Imai et al., 2010; Rudolph et al., 2019).

The problem addressed by this study is therefore the limited understanding of how DigiCow participation and information use relate to cattle health management, dairy productivity and cattle-related income within the evaluation sample. The study will build on, rather than duplicate, the existing evaluation by testing the robustness of estimated participation effects, investigating the management practice pathway, and analysing the information-use factors associated with reported practice change. Its contribution depends on access to the underlying evaluation microdata rather than published aggregate tables alone.

## 1.3 Objectives of the study

### 1.3.1 General objective

To assess the effects of DigiCow participation on dairy productivity and cattle-related net income among sampled smallholder farmers in Kenya, examine the potential explanatory role of cattle health management practices, and analyse information-use factors associated with reported practice change.

### 1.3.2 Specific objectives

1. To estimate the effect of DigiCow participation on milk production per cow, milk sales and annual net income from cattle among sampled smallholder farmers in Kenya.
2. To assess the effect of DigiCow participation on cattle health management practices and examine the extent to which these practices may explain the estimated relationship between participation and net income from cattle.
3. To examine the associations of the type and intensity of DigiCow information use, and of farmer characteristics, with the adoption of recommended dairy practices and reported production change among DigiCow users.

Milk sales will be kept as a separate outcome only if the dataset contains a suitable measure. Units, recall periods and the income definition will be aligned with the evaluation questionnaire and codebook before analysis.

## 1.4 Hypotheses

The study will test the following null hypotheses:

1. H~01~: DigiCow participation has no statistically significant effect on milk production per cow, milk sales or annual net income from cattle.
2. H~02a~: DigiCow participation has no statistically significant effect on the cattle health management practice index.
   H~02b~: Cattle health management practices are not significantly associated with net income from cattle, conditional on DigiCow participation and selected covariates.
3. H~03~: The type and intensity of DigiCow information use and selected farmer characteristics are not jointly associated with the adoption of recommended dairy practices or with reported production change among DigiCow users.

Formal hypotheses on indirect effects will be specified only if the data and identification assumptions support causal mediation analysis. Otherwise, the second objective will be addressed through explicitly exploratory pathway and attenuation analyses.

## 1.5 Justification of the study

### 1.5.1 Relevance to digital livestock service providers

The study will generate evidence that DigiCow and similar providers can use to understand the relationship between service participation, information use and farmer outcomes. Distinguishing access from active use and reported application may help providers identify where farmers disengage and which information categories deserve further investigation. The gap between access, active use and reported practice change in the existing evaluation makes this distinction particularly relevant (IDinsight, 2026). Associations identified in this study will inform service design questions but will not be interpreted as proof that a particular feature causes better outcomes.

### 1.5.2 Relevance to extension policy and programme design

County livestock departments, development partners and dairy cooperatives are intended users of the findings. The study may inform decisions on how digital advisory can complement public extension, veterinary services and farmer organisations. This is consistent with KASEP, which emphasises pluralistic service delivery, coordination, knowledge management and the use of digital technologies (MoALD, 2023). The findings will provide context-specific evidence rather than a national prescription for replacing face-to-face services.

### 1.5.3 Contribution to knowledge

The study will contribute to agricultural information and communication research by examining the relationship between information access, information use, management behaviour and economic outcomes in a livestock setting. Its contribution lies in combining an assessment of participation effects with exploratory pathway analysis and user-level analysis of practice adoption. An important analytical contribution will be to distinguish management practices from actual animal health outcomes, and explanatory associations from causally identified mechanisms. This distinction is necessary because the DigiCow index comprises reported practices, while causal mediation requires assumptions beyond those needed to estimate an overall participation effect (Imai et al., 2010).

### 1.5.4 Feasibility and responsible data use

Using existing evaluation data reduces fieldwork costs and avoids repeat interviews with farmers. The data-sharing package consists of de-identified datasets protected through k-anonymisation, with quasi-identifiers generalised (Mercy Corps AgriFin, n.d.-a). Feasibility nevertheless depends on access to the required respondent-level variables, adequate documentation and permission for the proposed secondary analysis. University ethical requirements and the data custodian's conditions will be followed, and no attempt will be made to identify respondents.

## 1.6 Scope and limitations of the study

### 1.6.1 Scope

The study will focus on farmers included in the DigiCow component of the ADF2 evaluation in Kenya: 502 DigiCow farmers and 775 comparison farmers interviewed in August 2025 (IDinsight, 2026). The final analytical sample will depend on data access, eligibility criteria, missing data and the overlap between participating and comparison farmers. The study will not be presented as nationally representative of Kenyan dairy farmers.

The analysis will cover participation status, available measures of milk production and sales, net income from cattle, cattle health management practices, information use and selected farmer characteristics. Participation-effect analyses will use eligible participating and comparison farmers, while information-use analyses will focus on DigiCow users. No primary data collection is proposed.

### 1.6.2 Selection bias and causal interpretation

Participation in DigiCow was not randomly assigned. The evaluation used propensity score matching and had no baseline survey (IDinsight, 2026). Causal interpretation therefore depends on whether observed pre-participation characteristics adequately account for selection, and on sufficient comparability between groups. Unobserved differences, such as motivation or management ability, may remain. Balance diagnostics, alternative specifications and sensitivity analyses will assess robustness but cannot prove that all selection bias has been removed.

### 1.6.3 Measurement and recall error

Outcomes were collected through telephone interviews. Income, production and usage measures may therefore be affected by recall error, reporting error and social desirability bias. Reported practice change differs from independently observed adoption, and reported production change differs from a consistently measured productivity outcome. These measures will be analysed and interpreted separately.

### 1.6.4 Limitations of the pathway analysis

The cattle health index combines management practices rather than direct clinical indicators of animal health, so it cannot by itself establish changes in disease incidence, mortality or physiological condition. The timing of the measures may also not establish that management changes preceded income changes. Formal mediation will therefore remain conditional on the data structure and the plausibility of the identification assumptions, and attenuation findings will be described as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence of a mechanism (Imai et al., 2010; Rudolph et al., 2019).

### 1.6.5 Information use and service-component attribution

The type and intensity of information use are not randomly assigned. Farmers who use DigiCow more often may differ from other users in resources, motivation, digital skills or production challenges, so these relationships will be described as associations. Furthermore, because DigiCow combines digital records, training, reminders and service-provider linkages, outcomes cannot easily be attributed to the smartphone application or to any single component (DigiCow Africa Ltd., n.d.).

### 1.6.6 Data availability, anonymisation and generalisability

The datasets are k-anonymised at k = 5, with variables such as age and location generalised (Mercy Corps AgriFin, n.d.-a). This may restrict detailed geographical and demographic analysis. Published tables alone will not support new respondent-level matching or mediation analysis, so the proposed analyses are conditional on access to the necessary microdata and documentation. Findings will apply mainly to the sampled farmers, service configuration and evaluation period.
